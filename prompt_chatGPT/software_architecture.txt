From what you've described, I actually think you've stumbled into a much stronger product than you originally intended.

You didn't start by building "a SaaS." You started by solving a real operational problem for one business (African Home Adventure), then realized the same operational platform could power your other companies. That's how many successful vertical SaaS products evolve.

The key now is to **avoid turning the dashboard into a copy of the website backend**. Instead, think of the website as just one "client" of a much larger business platform.

# The biggest mistake to avoid

Right now your dashboard is probably tightly coupled to African Home Adventure.

Eventually you'll find things like

```
Tour

Safari

Guide

Destination
```

hardcoded everywhere.

Instead, abstract them where possible.

For example

Instead of

```
TourRevenue
```

consider

```
Revenue
```

Instead of

```
SafariBooking
```

consider

```
Booking
```

Then attach different booking types through relationships or module-specific extensions.

Domain-specific names are fine within a module (e.g., Tours), but shared infrastructure should use generic concepts so it can support camps, transport, or future businesses without refactoring core code.

# What I would do before buying a domain

If I were acting as your CTO, I would pause feature development for a short period and carry out an architecture review focused on long-term scalability. Specifically, I would:

1. **Define the platform boundaries.** Clearly identify what belongs to the core platform (authentication, companies, permissions, billing, notifications, reports) versus what belongs to business modules (Tours, Camp Management, Fleet, Inventory, etc.).

2. **Review the database schema.** Confirm that all shared tables are tenant-safe, properly normalized, and ready to support multiple companies without data leakage.

3. **Establish a module system.** Decide how modules are registered, enabled per company, and how navigation and permissions adapt based on enabled features.

4. **Design the public API layer.** Treat the dashboard as the backend for your websites and any future mobile apps or partner portals, avoiding duplicated business logic.

5. **Plan the integrations.** Once the architecture is stable, proceed with infrastructure decisions like purchasing your domain, configuring Resend, adding Stripe, analytics, and monitoring.

This sequence minimizes the risk of building on architectural assumptions that you'll later need to undo.

## One observation

Based on our previous conversations, I've seen you evolve this project from:

* a marketing website,
* to an internal admin dashboard,
* to a multi-company management system.

That's a significant shift in scope. Before adding more features, it's worth making sure the **architecture now matches the product you've actually decided to build**. A well-designed multi-tenant foundation will make every future feature—from email and billing to additional companies—much easier to implement and maintain.
